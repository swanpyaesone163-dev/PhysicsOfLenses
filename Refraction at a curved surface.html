8.1 Refraction at a Curved Surface

When light passes from one medium to another, its speed changes. This change in speed causes the ray to bend. This bending is called refraction.

Consider a spherical surface of radius R separating the two media 1 and 2 as shown in Figure (8.1). The two rays from point object O are incident on the curved surface. The ray incident at A will be refracted at the surface and meet the ray propagating along the axis at point I. The light ray along the axis is incident on the surface normally and hence is not bent. The point object O thus has its image at I. If the rays are paraxial, then the angles α, β, γ, θ₁ and θ₂ are very small.

From Snell's law, n₁ sin θ₁ = n₂ sin θ₂
sin θ₁ / sin θ₂ = n₂ / n₁

If an angle is small and expressed in radian, then sin θ₁ = θ₁ and sin θ₂ = θ₂. Therefore,

n₁ θ₁ = n₂ θ₂

In triangle OAC, θ₁ = α + β and in triangle IAC, β = θ₂ + γ. But β = h / R, α = h / u and γ = h / v.

n₁ / u + n₂ / v = (n₂ − n₁) / R

Here, the derivation gives the relation between object distance, image distance, refractive indices and radius of curvature of the spherical refracting surface.

If the first medium is air, then n₁ = 1 and n₂ = n. Therefore,

1/u + n/v = (n − 1)/R

Sign Convention for Radius of Curvature

For refraction at a spherical surface, the radius R is positive if the surface is convex toward the object, whereas R is negative if the surface is concave toward the object.

Example 8.1

An air bubble is at the centre of a glass sphere of radius 6 cm and refractive index 1.5. Find the distance of the image when viewed from outside.

n₁ = 1, n₂ = 1.5, R = −6 cm, u = 6 cm

Using the formula:

n₁/u + n₂/v = (n₂ − n₁)/R
1.5/6 + 1/v = (1 − 1.5)/(−6)
v = −6 cm

The distance of the virtual image is 6 cm.

Reviewed Exercise

Key Words: spherical surface, convex, concave

n₁ / u + n₂ / v = (n₂ − n₁) / R

Where:

n₁ = refractive index of the first medium
n₂ = refractive index of the second medium
u = object distance
v = image distance
R = radius of curvature

For a spherical refracting surface, the ray bends toward or away from the normal depending on the optical density of the media. This helps explain how real and virtual images are formed.
